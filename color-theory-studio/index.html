How color works

Color is not a property of paint. It is what happens when light leaves a source, is filtered by a surface, lands on three kinds of sensor in your eye and is compared by your brain. Every rule in the other labs follows from these four steps, so start here.

1

Light is a mix of wavelengths, and your eye reads it with three sensors

Drag the wavelength. The curves show how strongly each cone type responds: S for short, M for medium, L for long wavelengths. Your brain never receives the wavelength, only these three numbers.

S conesM conesL cones

What follows from this. Three sensors mean any color is fully described by three numbers. That is why three well-chosen primaries can mix most colors, why screens need only red, green and blue, and why hue, value and saturation are enough to name any color.
2

Physically different light can be the same color

The left patch is one yellow wavelength, 580 nm. The right patch contains no yellow light at all, only green (540 nm) and red (620 nm). Slide until the cone responses match.

3

A surface can only subtract from the light that hits it

Paint has no color in the dark. A pigment absorbs some wavelengths and sends back the rest. Choose a light, then a paint, and watch what is left to reach your eye.

4

Wheel and harmony

Drag around the wheel to pick a hue. The dots show which other hues work with it under the scheme you choose.

Why opposites behave this way. Your brain codes hue on opposing channels, red against green and blue against yellow. A hue and its opposite push those channels in opposite directions, so side by side they exaggerate each other and mixed together they cancel. The wheel is a map of that system. The two wheels disagree slightly because each is an approximation built from a different set of primaries.

Why the paint mix looks like that

Each curve shows how much of each wavelength a paint reflects. A mixture can only send back light that both paints let through, so the mix curve sits low wherever either parent absorbs. Blue and yellow make green because green is the only band neither one fully absorbs. Compare the Clean green and Dull green pairs: the more the two curves overlap in the green band, the brighter the green.

Value and saturation

Value is how light or dark a color is. It carries the structure of a picture, and hue only decorates it. Train value first and everything else gets easier.

Why value comes first. Of the brain's three channels, the light and dark one carries nearly all the fine detail, edges and sense of form. The two hue channels are much blurrier. A picture with correct values and wrong hues still reads. A picture with lovely hues and wrong values does not.

Contrast and temperature

A color is never seen alone. Its neighbors change how light, dark, bright and warm it looks. This is the skill that separates a flat painting from a convincing one.

Simultaneous contrast

The center of every tile below is exactly the same color. Check the strip underneath.

Why it happens. Nerve cells in the eye and brain report the difference between a spot and its surroundings, not the absolute amount of light. Each surround pushes the center the opposite way on all three channels at once: lighter or darker, redder or greener, bluer or yellower.

Light on form

The color you paint is never the object's own color. On the lit side it is the object's color multiplied by the color of the light. In shadow it is the object's color multiplied by whatever fills the shadow, usually the sky or the room. Set the light and watch your color change.

    Principles only matter if they change what you do at the easel. Here is an order of decisions for starting a picture, a diagnosis guide for when one is not working, and a method for matching any color.

    Five decisions, in this order

    Each decision narrows the next one. Most color trouble comes from making decision four before decisions one and two.

    What is going wrong?

    Pick the symptom. You get the cause, traced back to how color works, and what to do about it.

    Match any color in three moves

    1. Value first. Mix until a dab of your paint disappears against the target when you squint. This is the hardest move and the one that matters most.
    2. Then hue. Ask which way the target leans around the wheel compared with your mix, and add a little of the neighbor on that side.
    3. Then saturation. If your mix is too bright, add a touch of its complement. If it is too dull, you cannot fix it by adding more paint. Start again with fewer, purer pigments.

    Your path

    Eight levels, starting from how color works and each built on the one before. The labs show you the idea and the exercises make it yours. Paint or draw with real materials as often as you can. Twenty minutes, three times a week, will take you further than one heroic Saturday.

    The mixing and drying models here are simplified approximations for learning. Your pigments, paper and screen will behave a little differently, so treat every result as a hypothesis to test with real paint.
